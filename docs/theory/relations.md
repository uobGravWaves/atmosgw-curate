# Physical relations and their sources

Each physical relation used in the code is listed here with its source, and is
cited in the code by its ID (e.g. `[R1]`). Status says whether the relation and
any numbers taken from the source have been checked against the source itself.

| ID | Relation | Source | Used in | Status |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| R1 | U.S. Standard Atmosphere, 1976, up to 149 km: geopotential altitude (eqs 18, 19), seven layers of molecular-scale temperature linear in geopotential height (eq 23, table 4) and the hydrostatic pressure equations (33a, 33b) up to 86 km; tabulated pressures (Table I) from 86 to 149 km | U.S. Standard Atmosphere, 1976, NOAA-S/T 76-1562 / NASA-TM-X-74335, U.S. Government Printing Office, Washington, D.C., October 1976 (NASA Technical Reports Server 19770009539) | `atmosphere.py` (and the MATLAB `h2p.m` and `p2h.m`) | Checked against the document on 2026-11-25: constants (table 2, p. 2; M0, p. 9), layer heights and gradients (table 4, p. 3), eqs (18), (19) (p. 8), (23) (p. 10), (33a), (33b) (p. 12), and the Table I pressures used above 86 km (p. 68, each checked against the table's P/P0 column). The equations reproduce the printed Table I pressures between -5 and 86 km to their 5 digits. Above 86 km the standard's pressure comes from a diffusion model (eq 33c); log-pressure interpolation between the tabulated values is the group's choice. Before 2026-11-25 the code used only the seven base pressures, joined by exponentials (Q1). |
| R2 | HIRDLS geolocation: profile positions are given at 30 km, and the measurement point moves about 0.6 km along track for each 1 km of vertical scan | Wright, C. J., Osprey, S. M., and Gille, J. C.: Global distributions of overlapping gravity waves in HIRDLS data, Atmos. Chem. Phys., 15, 8459-8477, https://doi.org/10.5194/acp-15-8459-2015, 2015; Sect. 4.3 | `obs/_limb_hirdls.py` | The 0.6 km and 30 km values were checked in Sect. 4.3 (2026-11-24). Applying the shift in opposite directions for upward and downward scans is not described in the paper; it is the group's own reasoning, carried over from the MATLAB loader. C. Wright confirmed (2026-11-24) that the retrieval's vertical regridding mentioned in Sect. 4.3 does not already move the positions, so the correction is not counted twice. |
| R3 | In a hydrostatic layer of constant (molecular-scale) temperature, pressure falls exponentially with geopotential altitude H: P = P_b exp[-g0' M0 (H - H_b) / (R* T_M,b)], so log(pressure) is linear in H, and interpolating log(pressure) linearly between levels is exact within such a layer | U.S. Standard Atmosphere, 1976 (as R1), eq (33b), p. 12: the form of the hydrostatic pressure equation for layers with zero gradient L_M,b of molecular-scale temperature (derived there from eq (5) with g dZ = g0' dH and M/T = M0/T_M) | `obs/limb.py` | Eq (33b) and its conditions checked against the document on 2026-11-25 (before then the source was a textbook reference, UNVERIFIED). Using it to interpolate pressure between levels (Q6 fix) is the group's own choice. Two approximations, both the group's own derivation, not from the standard: (1) the code interpolates in geometric height z, in which log(pressure) is not exactly linear even for an isothermal layer, because g decreases with height; the largest relative pressure error between two levels h apart is about h^2 / (4 H_s (r0 + z)), with scale height H_s = R* T / (g0' M0) and r0 = 6356.766 km: about 5e-6 for h = 1 km and 6e-5 for h = 3 km (checked numerically for 200-270 K, 30-80 km); (2) where temperature varies between levels, the result is approximate, but much closer than linear interpolation in height (see Q6). |
